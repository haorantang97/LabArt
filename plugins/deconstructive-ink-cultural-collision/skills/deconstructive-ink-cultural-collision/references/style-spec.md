# Executable style specification

## Shared ink grammar

## Ordered cun search

Treat a changing sequence of related brush decisions, not the individual line or a repeated packet, as the smallest structural unit:

1. begin with a short stroke that declares force or turn
2. answer it with unequal strokes that approach, diverge from, or stop before it
3. interrupt the sequence with dry gaps or paper notches
4. change brush face, loading, scale, or direction before the sequence becomes a pattern
5. return later with pale or dry crossing strokes that partially revise the first reading

Keep a shared force within a local sequence, but do not make the marks parallel or countable. Change length, pressure, loading, speed, spacing, and ending. Neighboring searches may cross at oblique angles, overlap, or dissolve into one another. They must never become repetitive hatching, water ripples, feathers, scales, trees, or decorative texture.

Use packets to describe forces rather than surfaces:

- compression: short crowded packets with blunt endings
- extension: sparse lengthened packets that become dry and lose contact
- rotation: several changing tangents, never a continuous ring
- cavity: paper reserve approached by interrupted packets from different sides
- hinge: layered compact dots and short turns, not a black filled joint
- atmosphere: only a few pale, visibly brushed passages; never a stain field

## Five-pass tonal budget

The following values describe work allocation, not rigid visible percentages:

- ghost scaffold, 10%: very pale, discontinuous, relational
- ordered construction, 45%: the largest amount of labor and micro-detail, with different brush character by region
- crossing revision, 25%: partial correction and concealment through repeated pale-gray ink, not blur
- accumulated hinges, 15%: localized repeated ink deposits
- pale consolidation, 5%: a few unifying brush passages

If the result feels empty, add more structurally specific brush searches, not washes. If it feels too explicit, cover selected local descriptions with a second or third pale-gray brush layer, not blur. If it feels too dark, preserve small paper notches and pale intervals rather than applying global haze.

## Regional hierarchy

- Recognition anchor: one or two short decisive strokes surrounded by less certain responses.
- Load-bearing field: the densest organized brush searches and accumulated gray values.
- Transition field: fewer crossing searches with more paper between them.
- Cavity or separation: irregular reserve formed by strokes stopping before it; never a clean outlined hole.
- Outer edge: only a consequence of internal searches reaching different distances. Do not paint the silhouette itself.

### Recognition before dissolution

Choose 3-5 anchors only. Examples: a horn direction and crouched weight; a launch plume and rising cylindrical body; a vertebral axis and rib field; a central landscape aperture and rotating ridge. Treat anchors as relationships between ink masses, not finished parts. Keep the overall relation legible while allowing each local description to remain provisional.

The target is not low detail. The target is **high labor of description with low certainty of description**. Place many specific, controlled marks, but let no single mark fully explain a plate, bone, muscle, window, or shadow. Details should overlap, absorb slightly into paper, abrade, and lose their names while still contributing to the whole. Do not use splashing or uncontrolled wet effects to manufacture uncertainty.

Target a hierarchy rather than an exact visual ratio:

- broad field: pale-to-mid gray mass, atmosphere, and large weight
- middle structure: directional cun families that carry anatomy, terrain, or mechanical load
- micro topology: broken tips, dots, hooks, short fibers, rubbed gaps, overlapping fragments
- focal knots: very few near-black hinges

Never substitute photo grain, generic paper texture, blur, or low contrast for micro topology.

### Stroke behavior

Use strokes with visible decisions: loaded start, pressure change, centered or side-brush travel, split tip, measured dry drag, lifted ending, partial overpaint, or controlled loss into paper. Long motions must be assembled from unequal but related segments, not a single vector-clean path. Keep stroke families legible as intentional brush order. Do not run repeated arcs around a volume. Do not let the brush behave like a topographic contour generator, fingerprint generator, water-ripple filter, or splatter brush.

Mix dry and moist ink locally, with restrained water:

- dry and semi-dry marks explain direction and friction
- controlled broken ink merges adjacent stroke families without blooming outward
- a thin, brushed pale layer lowers certainty and restores atmospheric unity
- concentrated ink fixes only the most important joints

Never use thrown ink, atomized spray, drips, runs, wet-on-wet cauliflower blooms, back-runs, puddled edges, or random blot fields. These belong to a different watercolor or splash-ink logic.

### Edge hierarchy

Use four edge states in one image:

1. short decisive anchor
2. hairy or split dry edge
3. damp feathered edge
4. missing edge replaced by paper reserve or neighboring strokes

No object may be enclosed by one continuous edge state.

### Tonal hierarchy

Stay monochrome. Warm paper is not a color accent. Preserve at least five functional values: paper, ghost gray, pale wash, structural gray, and dark knot. Avoid global contrast enhancement.

## Mode parameters

### Anatomical field

- Paper reserve: approximately 55-75%.
- Recognition anchors: body axis, one cavity, one joint cluster, one asymmetric paired structure.
- Detail: highest near joints and cavities; outer contour remains provisional.
- Let internal fields carry more information than the outer body edge. A rib-field is a family of abbreviated turns, not a row of bones.
- Allow a few delicate traces, but break, double, bury, or fade them.
- Avoid textbook labels, clean vascular diagrams, exact bilateral symmetry, and pen hatching.

### Modern colossus

- Paper/quiet field: approximately 25-45%, often carrying a thin gray veil rather than pure blank paper.
- Recognition anchors: monumental gesture, head direction, shoulder/torso weight, two or three limb vectors, one soft scale cue.
- Torso may be dark but must remain porous and multi-valued.
- Do not describe armor plates, sockets, pores, rivets, or manufactured surface components. Let mechanical character arise from posture and interrupted load paths.
- Use compressed packets at the shoulder and torso, lengthened dry packets through limbs, and sparse pale packets in the surrounding field.
- Limbs can stretch into pale dragged strokes; joints receive denser knots.
- Background masses must be blunt, abraded, and out of register. No crisp buildings.

### Rotational landscape

- Central or off-center paper aperture: approximately 10-25%.
- Build circulation from unequal families of short arcs, ridge strokes, hooked dots, rubbing, and layered wash.
- Vary speed and density around the orbit. Break every apparent ring.
- Let local passages read as terrain while the whole may suggest storm, eye, orbit, or geological force.
- Do not draw readable tree icons, rows of pines, scenic peaks, waterfalls, paths, or houses. Terrain must emerge from the aggregate behavior of cun and accumulated ink.
- Avoid smooth spiral filters, perfect circles, radial symmetry, and uniform particle fields.

### General modern subject

- Convert forces rather than surfaces: thrust, compression, recoil, rotation, strain, heat, or collapse become stroke directions.
- Retain only enough engineering geometry for recognition.
- Corrode all hard geometry through overlap, dry gaps, pale wash, and lost edges.
- Do not add traditional landscape motifs as shortcuts.

## Deconstruction, not structuralism

Keep enough structure for recognition while refusing a single final account of the object's parts. A rib can also behave like a mountain ridge; a machine joint can also behave like an ink knot; a plume can also behave like a vertical wash. This ambiguity must arise from shared brush behavior, not literal collage.

## Diagnostic failures

- Looks like an ink pen sketch: too many equally thin, stable lines.
- Looks like watercolor: clear contour plus translucent fill.
- Looks like charcoal concept art: value modeled by light and shadow instead of ink layering.
- Looks abstract but cannot be named: recognition anchors are missing.
- Looks empty: only broad washes exist, with no medium or micro stroke families.
- Looks noisy: micro marks are evenly distributed and carry no directional force.
- Looks engineered: long lines are straight, parallel, and evenly spaced.
- Looks AI-stylized: the complete subject remains clearly modeled beneath repeating water-wave bands, fingerprint whorls, scalloped arcs, designed holes, or decorative ink erosion.
- Looks detailed but not painted: every local part is explicit and nameable instead of being found and lost through successive ink passes.
- Looks like Western expressive watercolor: random splashes, droplets, drips, wet blooms, pooled edges, broad accidental stains, or chaotic brush collisions dominate the image.
- Looks uncontrolled: marks have no shared direction, sequence, or structural role even when the subject remains recognizable.
- Looks washed out: ambiguity comes from weak global contrast or blur while ordered micro-detail is absent.
- Looks patterned: one brush shape repeats across the whole image without regional variation.
- Looks conventionally illustrated: trees, rocks, peaks, ribs, panels, or tower parts are itemized instead of being absorbed into the ink field.
- Looks kitschy: modern subject is literally combined with mountains or decorative tradition symbols.

## Calibration discipline

Calibration images are evidence of material behavior, not content templates. Extract only:

- relative scale of broad, middle, and micro brushwork
- how repeated ink layers retain visible stroke history
- how a locally detailed field becomes uncertain without blur
- how paper reserve enters and separates ink fields
- how dark passages preserve internal gray and dry gaps

Never transfer:

- exact composition or value map
- recognizable subject, pose, anatomy, landscape opening, or silhouette
- inscriptions, seals, borders, mounting, or photographic color cast
- a unique local cluster of marks as a copied motif

If the generated composition resembles a calibration image before considering medium, reject it as over-referenced.
