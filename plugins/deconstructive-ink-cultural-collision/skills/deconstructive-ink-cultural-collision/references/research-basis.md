# Research basis

## Broken ink

The Palace Museum describes broken ink as introducing dark ink into pale ink before it dries, or the reverse, enriching ink variation while breaking the boundary of the depicted object.

Source: https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/242650.html

## Splashed ink

The Palace Museum describes splashed ink as relying on broad gesture, rendering, and the natural diffusion of water and ink to form images.

Source: https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/242649.html

## Layered ink

The Palace Museum defines layered ink as gradual staining from pale to dark, producing depth through repeated deposits rather than photometric shading.

Source: https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/240580.html

## Ink carries many colors

The Palace Museum explains "five colors of ink" as rich variation in concentration, dilution, wetness, dryness, and blackness, not literal color or fixed flat swatches.

Source: https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/240484.html

## Water and brush

Nankai University's discussion of expressive Chinese painting emphasizes that water quantity, brush pressure, speed, direction, paper absorbency, water-leading-ink, water-breaking-ink, and ink-breaking-water jointly create ink behavior.

Source: https://art.nankai.edu.cn/2013/0629/c11137a112357/page.htm

## Light and shadow

The Metropolitan Museum of Art notes that traditional Chinese painters often rejected changeable light and shadow as a means of modeling, relying instead on the inked brush.

Source: https://www.metmuseum.org/de/essays/chinese-painting
